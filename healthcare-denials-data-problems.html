Healthcare Denials Are Often Data Problems, Not Billing Problems

By Soledad Banner — The Queen of RCM
September 25, 2026

Healthcare organizations love to treat denials like a billing problem.

A claim denies, it drops into a work queue, someone fixes it, resubmits it, and everyone moves on.

But a lot of denials have very little to do with billing.

The actual problem may have started days earlier with bad eligibility data, an authorization that never made it into the right system, a payer rule nobody knew changed, a registration error, or information that technically existed but never reached the person who needed it.

By the time the claim denies, you are often looking at the last visible failure in a much longer chain.

That distinction matters because if you only fix the claim, you have not necessarily fixed the problem.

Stop treating every denial like it is unique

One of the easiest ways to waste money in revenue cycle is to build a highly efficient process for fixing the same preventable error 500 times.

That happens all the time.

The denial team gets faster. The work queues get cleaner. Productivity improves.

Meanwhile, the same underlying issue keeps feeding new accounts into the queue.

A denial is not just an account that needs to be worked. It is also information.

It tells you that something failed somewhere in the process: a workflow, a system configuration, a data field, a payer rule, a handoff between teams, or a piece of technology that did not behave the way people assumed it would.

If the same denial reason keeps showing up, I want to know why. Not just how to fix it.

Eligibility is a perfect example

Eligibility-related denials often get written off as registration mistakes.

Sometimes they are.

But that is not the end of the investigation.

Was eligibility actually checked? Did the response contain useful information? Did the system interpret the response correctly? Was there a coverage change between scheduling and the date of service?

Did the payer return something ambiguous? Was the response buried somewhere staff could not easily see? Did the workflow rely on someone remembering to check three different systems?

Those are very different problems, even if they eventually produce the same denial code.

If you treat every one of them as “registration error,” you lose the opportunity to identify the actual failure point.

The useful question is where the pattern starts

RCM does not need another dashboard just for the sake of having a dashboard.

The useful question is whether the data can show you where the same failure is happening over and over again.

I want to see denials by payer, plan, location, provider, procedure, denial reason, authorization status, eligibility response, workflow step, and date of service.

Then I want to look for patterns.

Maybe one payer is returning incomplete benefit information for a particular plan.

Maybe one location has a spike in authorization denials because documentation is not reaching the right team.

Maybe a certain procedure started denying after a payer policy changed and nobody updated the workflow.

Maybe a specific insurance product is creating trouble because the system maps it incorrectly.

Those are not isolated billing problems.

They are operational problems showing up in claims data.

Healthcare technology is often part of the story

Revenue cycle does not happen in one system.

There may be an EHR, a practice management system, a clearinghouse, payer portals, eligibility tools, authorization platforms, spreadsheets, analytics tools, and a handful of manual workarounds holding the whole process together.

That creates a lot of places for information to get lost.

And one thing I have learned about healthcare technology is that a process can look automated while still depending on a surprising amount of manual interpretation.

That is where problems get interesting.

The technology may technically be working, but the workflow around it is not.

Or the workflow is fine, but the system is not surfacing the right information.

Or the payer data is inconsistent.

Or one platform is sending information that the next platform does not know what to do with.

By the time somebody notices, the claim is already denied.

Automation only helps if you understand the failure first

I am a big believer in automation.

I am also a big believer in not automating nonsense.

If you automate a broken workflow, you usually just create the same problem faster and at a larger scale.

Before automating a denial-related process, I want to understand:

  • where the information enters the workflow
  • who touches it
  • where it changes
  • where it disappears
  • what the downstream financial impact is
  • whether the same problem is happening repeatedly

Then automation can be useful.

At that point, you are not just making people click fewer buttons. You are using technology to reduce the chance that the error happens in the first place.

The real opportunity is prevention

Most denial teams are incredibly good at fixing problems after they happen.

The bigger opportunity is identifying what can be prevented before the claim goes out the door.

Not every denial is preventable.

But a lot of them are predictable.

Historical denial patterns, eligibility mismatches, missing authorizations, payer-specific rules, coding combinations, documentation gaps, and coverage changes all leave signals.

The question is whether the organization is looking at those signals early enough to do anything about them.

That is where revenue cycle, analytics, and healthcare technology start to overlap in a much more meaningful way.

Because the goal is not just to work denials faster.

It is to understand why they keep happening.

And then make fewer of them happen at all.
